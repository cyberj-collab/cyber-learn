Your first job

Getting Hired

You don't need years of experience to land your first cybersecurity-related job. You need proof that you can learn, solve problems, and be trusted. This page shows you how to build that proof and present it.

Every path is different. Many people start in IT help desk, networking, or system administration before moving into a security role, and that's a normal, respected route.

Common First Jobs

  • IT help desk or technical support. You learn how real systems and users work.
  • Junior SOC analyst (security operations center). You watch alerts and escalate real threats.
  • Network or system administrator (junior). You build the systems that security protects.
  • IT auditor or GRC assistant (governance, risk, and compliance). You check that policies and controls are followed.

These are examples, not the only routes. You might also search for titles like "Security Analyst I," "Cybersecurity Technician," and "Information Security Associate."

Build Proof Before You Apply

Employers want to see what you can do, not just what you've studied.

  • Home lab write-ups. Explain what you built, what broke, and how you fixed it. See the Labs Directory.
  • Platform progress. List completed TryHackMe paths, Hack The Box machines, or CTF results.
  • A simple portfolio. A free GitHub page or blog with a few clear project write-ups is a useful starting portfolio. Hiring requirements vary.
  • Certifications, if they fit your goal. Requirements vary by job. Some employers or contracts require particular certifications, and others don't. See the Cert Roadmap.

Protect privacy when you publish.

  • Follow each platform's rules about sharing answers. Write about what you learned, not the flags.
  • Remove real names, IP addresses, passwords, keys, and any employer or customer data from screenshots and write-ups.
  • Publish only material the platform and the system owner allow.

Your Resume

  • Aim for one page early in your career. That's common advice, not a strict rule.
  • Lead with skills and projects if you don't have security job experience yet.
  • Use the job posting's words, truthfully. Many employers use software to screen resumes, so match the skills you really have to the posting's wording. Keywords help, but they don't guarantee you'll get through.
  • Show results, not duties. Write "Built a home lab with 3 virtual machines and documented a phishing-analysis workflow" instead of "Interested in security."
  • Count your past jobs. Customer service, retail, and the military all build skills security teams value, like staying calm under pressure, clear communication, and following procedures.

Your LinkedIn Profile

  • Use a headline that says where you're headed, such as "Aspiring SOC Analyst | Security+ Candidate | Home Lab Builder."
  • Add your projects and certifications to the Featured and Licenses sections.
  • Share short posts about what you're learning. Consistency matters more than polish.

Networking Without the Awkwardness

  • Join communities. Local ISSA, ISACA, or OWASP chapters, BSides conferences, and Discord servers for the platforms you use.
  • Ask for advice, not jobs. "How did you get into your role?" starts better conversations.
  • Follow up. A short thank-you message helps people remember you.

Interview Prep

Example questions you might hear

  • "Walk me through what happens when you type a website address into a browser."
  • "What's the difference between a vulnerability, a threat, and a risk?"
  • "How would you respond if a user reported a suspicious email?"
  • "Tell me about a project you built and a problem you solved."
  • "How do you stay current in security?"

How to answer well

  • Use the STAR method for experience questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result.
  • It's okay to say "I don't know." Follow it with how you'd find the answer.
  • Bring a home lab or project story. It's often the most memorable part of an entry-level interview.

Watch Out for Scams

  • Real employers don't ask you to pay for training, equipment, or a background check to get hired.
  • Be wary of offers that arrive without an interview or that come only through chat apps.
  • Check that the company and recruiter are real before sharing personal details.

Next Steps

No page or plan can promise a job, but these steps make you a stronger candidate.

  1. Pick one first-job title from the list above.
  2. Find 5 real postings for it and note the skills they repeat.
  3. Build one project that shows one of those skills.
  4. Update your resume and LinkedIn with that project.
