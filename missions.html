Try a Mission

Practice with fictional examples. No downloads, account access, or sign-in secrets are needed.

Mission 1: The Unexpected Message

⏱ 5 minutesSkill: evaluate a suspicious request.
Fictional inbox messageFrom: School Help Desk support@campus-help.example Subject: Your access ends in 15 minutes Message: Your student account needs verification. Open the attached file, then reply with your password and the six-digit sign-in code. Do not contact the school office; they cannot help with this update.

This is a pretend message, so there is no real link or attachment.

Questions

  1. Select the warning signs: (A) pressure to act immediately; (B) request for a password and sign-in code; (C) discouraging independent verification; (D) the words “Help Desk” alone prove the sender is real.
  2. What is the best next step? (A) reply with only the password; (B) open the attachment to investigate; (C) contact school support through a known official route and report the message.
Show answers and explanations
  1. A, B, and C. Urgency, requests for sign-in secrets, and attempts to prevent verification are warning signs. A display name or official-sounding address is not proof of identity. These clues justify caution; no single cosmetic clue reliably identifies every phishing message.
  2. C. Independently verify through contact details you already trust. Follow your school's reporting process. Do not share passwords or one-time codes or open an unexpected attachment to test it.

Reflection: What would you do differently if you had already opened the attachment? Suggested discussion: stop interacting with it and promptly tell the school's IT/security team what happened; do not hide the mistake.

Mission 2: Read the Login Trail

⏱ 5 minutesSkill: distinguish evidence from assumptions.
Fictional sample logsAll times are relative within this fictional exercise; they are not real activity. 09:00 | user: learner1 | sign-in failed | source: A 09:01 | user: learner1 | sign-in failed | source: A 09:02 | user: learner1 | sign-in failed | source: A 09:03 | user: learner1 | sign-in successful | source: A 09:05 | user: learner2 | sign-in successful | source: B

Questions

  1. Which pattern deserves a closer look? (A) repeated failures followed by success for learner1; (B) any successful login automatically proves an attack.
  2. Does this trail prove learner1's account was compromised? (A) yes; (B) no, it needs more context.
  3. Choose useful follow-up evidence: (A) whether learner1 recognizes the activity; (B) device/session and multifactor records, where available to an authorized reviewer; (C) guessing the person's password to test it.
Show answers and explanations
  1. A. A success after failures can merit investigation, but an ordinary typing mistake can produce the same pattern.
  2. B. Logs record events, not the user's intent. The sample does not establish who was using the account.
  3. A and B. Authorized reviewers can compare evidence and ask the account owner. Never attempt to sign into someone else's account.

Reflection: Write one sentence separating an observation from a conclusion. Example: “Three failed sign-ins preceded a success; I need more evidence before calling it a compromise.”

Mission 3: Give a Fictional Account a Checkup

⏱ 5 minutesSkill: choose stronger account protections.
Fictional starting settingsPassword: reused on another service (no actual password displayed). Multifactor authentication: off. Recovery email: old and inaccessible. Available options: passkey, authenticator app, recovery codes.

Questions

  1. Which change reduces password-reuse risk? (A) a unique password stored in a password manager; (B) changing one character in the reused password.
  2. Which available sign-in option is designed to resist phishing? (A) a passkey; (B) sharing a one-time code with anyone claiming to be support.
  3. What should happen to recovery settings? (A) keep an inaccessible email; (B) update it to an account you control and protect, and store recovery codes securely.
Show answers and explanations
  1. A. Unique passwords reduce the damage when a different service's password leaks. A password manager helps generate and keep track of them.
  2. A. Passkeys are phishing-resistant. An authenticator app is another useful multifactor option when a passkey is unavailable, but its codes can still be phished. Never share sign-in codes with someone who contacts you.
  3. B. Recovery needs to work when you lose normal access. Treat recovery codes as secrets and follow the service's storage guidance.

This is a simulated checklist, not an account setup form. Cyber-Learn must not collect visitors' passwords, codes, or recovery secrets.
