Ethics

Where It's Legal to Practice

Learning security skills on systems you're authorized to use is generally fine. Testing systems without authorization can break the law, contracts, or platform rules. This page explains the difference so you can practice with confidence.

This page is general guidance for learners, not legal advice. Laws differ by country and state.

The One Rule

Only test systems when you have clear, written authorization that covers exactly which targets, which actions, and what time window.

Owning a computer isn't always enough. If your system runs on a cloud provider, a host, or anyone else's service, their rules apply too. Everything else on this page follows from that rule.

Safe Places to Practice

  • Training platforms like TryHackMe, Hack The Box, picoCTF, and OverTheWire. Their targets are built for you to attack, and you agree to their rules when you sign up.
  • Your own home lab, meaning virtual machines you set up on your own computer and keep on an isolated network. See the Labs Directory.
  • Capture-the-flag (CTF) competitions, which give you permission for the specific targets in scope.
  • Bug bounty programs such as those on HackerOne or Bugcrowd, but only for the targets and testing methods each program lists as in scope. Read the program rules before you start.

Not Okay, Even "Just to Learn"

  • Scanning or attacking your school, employer, or a friend's network without written permission.
  • Testing public websites, apps, or Wi-Fi networks without the owner's authorization. Something being public is not permission. You need authorization and a clear scope, either from an official bug bounty program or directly from the owner.
  • Logging into accounts that aren't yours, even if you guessed or found the password.
  • Running live malware as a beginner. Use safe, simulated samples instead. Real malware analysis needs a purpose-built, controlled environment and expert guidance.

Why It Matters

In the United States, unauthorized access can violate the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), state computer-crime laws, contracts such as terms of service, and other laws. Other countries have their own laws, such as the UK's Computer Misuse Act. Good intentions don't count as permission. This page can't tell you whether a specific activity is legal; when it matters, ask a qualified professional.

Good Habits from Day One

  • Get permission in writing, and know exactly what's in scope.
  • Follow each platform's rules. For example, many platforms ask you not to publish answers or write-ups for active challenges.
  • Keep vulnerable machines contained. Put them on a dedicated internal network in your home lab, with no extra NAT or bridged adapters, no routing, and no shared folders or clipboard. See the Labs Directory.
  • When in doubt, don't. Ask the platform, the program owner, or an instructor first.

Employers look for people they can trust. Practicing ethically from the start is part of becoming hireable.
